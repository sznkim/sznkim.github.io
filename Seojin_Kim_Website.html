About

I am an MPH candidate at Seoul National University’s Graduate School of Public Health and a researcher in the Health Economics I Lab. My research focuses on household responses to health shocks: how families adjust healthcare use, caregiving, work, and financial resources, and how health and social policies alter those responses. I am particularly interested in older households and couples as settings in which health risks, care responsibilities, and economic consequences extend beyond the individual directly affected. Methodologically, I work with panel and administrative data and seek to develop rigorous causal research designs for dynamic and heterogeneous policy effects.

Policy Development and Challenges in Pediatric Palliative Care in South Korea: A Policy Triangle Analysis

Seojin Kim*, Sujeong Kim† · Korean Society for Hospice and Palliative Care Summer Conference, Gwangju · Poster presentation.

Abstract

Background: Pediatric palliative care in South Korea has expanded through five programs introduced since 2018, but the programs were developed separately for different populations and service needs. This study examined institutional gaps and limitations in coordination across the programs.

Methods: Walt and Gilson’s policy triangle was applied to analyze policy content, context, actors, and processes. Two researchers compared 15 government and public-agency documents published from 2016 to 2026 using a structured framework.

Results: The five programs differed in eligibility, service scope, financing, and administrative structure. Referral and transition mechanisms were limited; the legal framework did not clearly address pediatric patients; and financing was divided between pilot health-insurance fees and government subsidies. Although families are central to care decisions, they were not formally identified as participants in policy design or evaluation. Designated institutions were limited and concentrated in tertiary hospitals in the Seoul metropolitan area.

Conclusion: Korea has expanded pediatric palliative-care capacity, but institutional inconsistencies constrain continuity of care. Clearer legal foundations, cross-program coordination, information sharing, integrated evaluation, and formal family participation are needed.

03May 15–16, 2025

The Impact of Diabetes-Related Knowledge and Motivation on Self-Care Management among Adult Diabetic Patients

Seojin Kim*, S. Kim, et al. · 50th Korean Public Health Joint Academic Conference · Poster presentation.

Abstract

Background: Diabetes knowledge is important for self-management, but information alone may not produce sustained behavior change. This study examined the relationships among diabetes knowledge, personal motivation, social motivation, and self-care among adults with type 2 diabetes in Siheung City.

Methods: A cross-sectional survey was conducted with 68 adults aged 40 years or older who visited a general hospital or public health center in July 2024. Diabetes knowledge, personal motivation, social motivation, and weekly self-care were measured using validated instruments. Descriptive statistics, independent-samples t-tests, Pearson correlations, and multiple regression were conducted in SPSS 29.

Results: Social motivation was positively associated with blood-glucose management (β = 0.32, p = .010), whereas diabetes knowledge was not directly associated with overall self-care (p = .341). Women reported higher personal motivation than men, and respondents not living with a spouse reported higher self-care. Knowledge was positively related to personal motivation but did not independently predict self-care.

Conclusion: Diabetes-management interventions should move beyond information provision and incorporate personal motivation, family education, peer support, and community-based support networks.

04Feb 7–8, 2025

Economic Evaluations of Telehealth Interventions for Children and Adolescents

Seojin Kim*, Jimin Oh, Yeonji Jung · AUA Postgraduate Academic Forum: Global Health in the Era of Digital Transformation · Poster presentation.

Abstract

Background: Telehealth can reduce temporal and geographic barriers to healthcare for children and adolescents, yet evidence on its economic value in these populations remains limited.

Methods: We systematically searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and EMBASE for studies published from January 2014 through November 2024. Three reviewers independently screened studies in accordance with PRISMA. Eligible studies involved participants younger than 18 years, active provider participation, and at least one economic evaluation. Methodological quality was assessed with the SPOR CiCERO Checklist.

Results: Eight of 1,100 identified studies met the inclusion criteria. Six evaluated internet-based or remote interventions, and five reported that telehealth was cost-effective or generated savings relative to in-person care. Benefits were driven by lower travel and caregiver productivity costs and improved access. Limitations included weak study designs, nonstandardized cost measurement, and limited economic modeling.

Conclusion: Telehealth can be a financially efficient alternative for pediatric and adolescent care, particularly where access is limited. Future research should use standardized economic methods, direct quality-of-life measures, and longer-term assessments of sustainability and scalability.

05Nov 25–26, 2024

Addressing Vulnerabilities in Home-Based Care During Disasters

Seojin Kim* · 15th Asia Pacific Conference on Disaster Medicine · Poster presentation.

Abstract

Aims: This study identified disaster-related risks faced by older adults, children, and people with disabilities receiving home-based care and proposed preparedness strategies for care providers.

Methods: A literature review was conducted in PubMed and Scopus using terms related to home-based care, disasters, emergency preparedness, and vulnerable populations. The review focused on provider education, evacuation, and access to essential medical equipment.

Results: Vulnerable populations faced substantial evacuation barriers arising from mobility limitations, cognitive impairment, and dependence on continuously powered medical equipment such as ventilators. Case studies identified inadequate backup power, poorly tailored evacuation plans, insufficient provider training, and limited caregiver preparedness, particularly among low-income households.

Conclusion: Preparedness should include targeted provider education, collaboration with disaster-management authorities, population-specific response protocols, and emergency supply kits for people receiving care at home.

06Nov 6, 2024

Enhancing Primary Care for Adults with Autism Spectrum Disorder: The Role of Nurse Practitioners

Seojin Kim*, Huiyeon Lee · SNU Research Institute of Nursing Science International Nursing Research Virtual Conference · Poster presentation.

Abstract

Background: Adults with autism spectrum disorder face communication, sensory, financial, and organizational barriers in primary care. Many primary-care professionals report limited preparation for addressing their needs.

Methods: A narrative review of PubMed and the Cochrane Library examined studies concerning primary care for adults aged 19 years or older with autism spectrum disorder.

Results: Ten studies were included. Provider self-efficacy and confidence were consistently important: professionals with lower self-efficacy were less likely to adapt services to autistic patients’ needs, largely because of limited formal education and experience.

Conclusion: Strengthening autism-related education for nurse practitioners can support more individualized primary care and improve health outcomes among adults with autism spectrum disorder.
